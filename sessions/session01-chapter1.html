Chapter 1: Ghost Ship

USS Enterprise, NCC-1701 — Stardate 2267.143

The turbolift doors opened and T’Karra stepped onto the bridge of the USS Enterprise.

She had read the specifications, of course. Every technical manual the Vulcan Science Academy possessed on Constitution-class vessels, every refit annotation, every engineering addendum filed since the ship’s launch. She knew the bridge was eleven point seven metres in diameter. She knew the overhead lighting cycled through four luminosity settings corresponding to the ship’s duty rotations. She knew that the central command chair could swivel three hundred and sixty degrees on a frictionless bearing.

None of that had prepared her for the smell.

Recycled air, but not the sterile variety she knew from Vulcan research stations. This air carried traces of the people who breathed it — coffee, metal polish, the faint musk of a dozen species who had sweated into these seats during alert drills and deep-space watches. The Enterprise smelled inhabited. Lived in. The scent of a ship that had stories ground into its deck plating.

T’Karra adjusted her glasses — an old habit, the frames perfectly fitted, the gesture purely psychological — and surveyed her new domain.

She was aware of the picture she presented. Vulcan robes, deep charcoal trimmed with copper thread, among a bridge full of Starfleet uniforms. No rank insignia. No combadge. No concession whatsoever to the military hierarchy that governed every other soul on this deck. She was twenty-three and looked it — young for a Vulcan, practically embryonic by some standards — but she had cultivated a bearing that suggested centuries of diplomatic training compressed into a frame that still carried a faint, almost scandalous flush of green-pink in her cheeks.

She raised one eyebrow and let the silence do its work.

The bridge crew of the B-shift — the night watch, the understudies, the officers who took the conn when Captain Kirk retired to his quarters — looked back at her with expressions ranging from mild bewilderment to active suspicion.

Her gaze found the science station first.

The young man sitting there wore the blue of the sciences division, a lieutenant junior grade by the braid on his sleeve. Human, though something about his posture suggested he hadn’t grown up among them. He sat with his shoulders slightly hunched, as though accustomed to being the smallest person in any room, and his fingers moved across his console with the practised fluency of someone who thought in equations. When he noticed T’Karra watching him, he didn’t startle. He simply looked back with an expression of quiet, analytical interest — one pattern-recognition engine assessing another.

“I am T’Karra,” she said. “Envoy. Diplomat.” She paused, letting the words settle into the ambient hum of the bridge. “I am curious about you.”

The young man — Vex, according to the duty roster she had memorised — tilted his head. “I am well,” he said carefully. “That depends on how much I’ve found to actually do.”

There was a directness to him that T’Karra found agreeable. No posturing, no performance of deference. He was precisely what his file suggested: a prodigy, raised on Tellar Prime among a species that valued bluntness, transplanted into Starfleet where his considerable intellect was somewhat undermined by a total absence of social grace.

“We could be friends,” she said.

Vex blinked. Across the bridge, someone made a sound that might have been a suppressed laugh.

“I also have none of this ‘rizz’ that humans speak of,” T’Karra added, and Vex’s mouth twitched into something that was not quite a smile but lived in the same neighbourhood.

She turned.

The Andorian was harder to ignore. He occupied the tactical station the way a predator occupies a ridgeline — every angle calculated, every sightline accounted for. Lieutenant Junior Grade Thon stood well over six feet, broad across the shoulders, with the dense musculature of someone who trained for combat the way other officers trained for promotions. His antennae swept in slow, continuous arcs, tracking movement, tracking threat. The phaser at his hip was a type-2 — heavier than standard bridge carry — and his hand rested near it with the unconscious ease of long practice.

His skin was the deep blue of Andorian polar ice, and his eyes held the same temperature.

“You seem on edge, Mister Thon,” T’Karra observed. “Any particular reason why?”

Thon looked at her. The assessment was unhurried and thorough — robes, glasses, the absence of rank, the absence of a weapon. T’Karra held his gaze. She could feel his judgement forming like weather: civilian, no training, no authority, no threat. His antennae dipped once, dismissive, and he looked away. Back to his sweep of the bridge. Back to his perimeter.

She filed the interaction away. He would be difficult. Andorians respected strength and chain of command in roughly equal measure, and she represented neither. She would need to earn Thon’s regard through competence, which was — she permitted herself the private observation — the only currency worth trading in.

On the far side of the bridge, someone shifted in the dim light of a peripheral console.

The Orion woman was not wearing a uniform either.

This was interesting.

T’Karra studied her with the careful attention she would give a diplomatic communique with hidden clauses. The woman sat with a dancer’s poise — relaxed, centred, dangerous in the way that a sheathed blade is dangerous. Her skin caught the bridge lighting and turned it into something liquid. She watched T’Karra with eyes that gave away precisely nothing while cataloguing everything.

Malevolant. Ensign, officially. Political liaison, like T’Karra herself. The file had been notably thin — deliberate gaps that spoke louder than the information they replaced.

They regarded each other across the bridge, two women whose job descriptions said diplomat and whose skill sets said something considerably more complicated, and for a moment the Enterprise bridge felt like a negotiation table with the terms still being written.

Neither spoke. Neither needed to. The conversation happened in the space between — I see you, I know what you are, we will discuss this later — and then T’Karra moved on.

She walked toward the command chair.

Not to it. Toward it. She stopped precisely one pace to its right and stood with her hands clasped behind her back, occupying the space the way Spock was rumoured to — present, authoritative, technically not sitting in the chair and therefore technically not claiming it.

It was, she had calculated, the exact posture most likely to provoke a reaction from the one crew member she had not yet addressed.

The cable caught her attention first. It ran from behind the engineering console in a lazy spiral, like the cord of some ancient Terran communication device, and terminated in a port set directly into the skull of the oldest person T’Karra had ever seen in active service. The Edosian had three arms, and at the moment two of them were engaged with the engineering console while the third — the one growing from the centre of his chest — operated a secondary display that appeared to be showing schematics for a mid-twentieth-century audio reproduction device.

Brex. Lieutenant. Four hundred years old, give or take a decade. He predated the Federation itself. He had been a starship engineer when Vulcan was still debating whether to make first contact with Earth.

His third hand rose from the secondary display, waved once in her direction without his eyes leaving the console, and said: “I don’t think you’re supposed to be sat there.”

“Says who?” T’Karra replied.

“Just the usual way of things.” Brex’s fingers — all nine of them across three hands — continued their work. His neural implant pulsed with a faint amber light where the cable met his temple. “Sit where you like. Doesn’t bother me.”

There was an entire philosophy in that response, T’Karra noted. Four centuries of institutional knowledge, distilled into indifference. Brex had seen enough commanding officers to fill a historical database. One more Vulcan with ideas about authority was, to him, weather. It would pass. The hi-fi would not fix itself.

“What are you working on?” she asked, genuine curiosity bleeding through the formal register of her voice.

“Capacitor diagram. Pioneer SX-1980 receiver, 1978. Humans made extraordinary things when they were still bound to a single planet.” He glanced at her over his shoulder, and for the first time she saw the full scope of his cybernetic implant — crude by modern standards, a thick disc of duranium-composite bonded to his temporal ridge with interface pins that disappeared into his skull. “Curious where he got his cable from,” Brex muttered, more to himself than to her, and went back to his console.

T’Karra permitted herself a moment of something that was not quite fondness.

“There is one more of you,” she said, turning.

The Kzinti had been watching from the periphery with the patience of a species that had spent millennia perfecting the art of the ambush. Healer — no rank prefix, no surname, just the word itself, offered like a promise and a boundary — stood near the medical station with her arms folded across her chest. She was smaller than the Kzinti T’Karra had studied in xenobiology texts, but she carried herself with the coiled stillness of something that could move very quickly when it chose to.

Her fur was tawny, her eyes amber and unblinking, and when she spoke, her voice carried the dry precision of someone who had decided, very deliberately, to be gentle.

“I’m Healer,” she said. “I’m here to make sure everybody stays well.”

There was a pause. The bridge hummed. Somewhere in the guts of the ship, the warp core turned matter into miracle with the steady patience of a heartbeat.

“Our society is changing,” Healer added, into the silence that followed. “Women are allowed to speak now.”

T’Karra processed this. The Kzinti Patriarchy had been, until recently, one of the most restrictive gender hierarchies in the known galaxy. That this woman stood on the bridge of a Federation starship in a medical officer’s uniform was not merely unusual — it was an act of revolution wearing sensible boots.

“That is impressive indeed,” T’Karra said, and meant it.

She turned back to face the viewscreen, the stars holding steady in their ancient positions, and clasped her hands behind her back.

“All stations report readiness.”

It was not a request. It was not quite an order. It occupied the diplomatic grey space between — a suggestion with the structural integrity of a command. T’Karra had spent her entire brief career in that grey space, and she navigated it the way Vex navigated quantum equations: instinctively, joyfully, and with absolute confidence in the underlying mathematics.

Brex reported first, his third hand still scrolling through capacitor diagrams. Vex followed, crisp and precise. Thon’s response was clipped, professional, and carried the subtext of I’m reporting to my station, not to you. Malevolant’s voice was silk over steel. Healer simply nodded.

T’Karra stood beside the captain’s chair of the USS Enterprise and looked at the crew that had been given to her — the prodigy, the warrior, the spy, the ancient, the predator-turned-healer — and thought: Curious.

The night shift had begun.

The bridge had settled into an uneasy equilibrium — six officers at six stations, the hum of the Enterprise’s engines filling the silence that none of them seemed willing to break — when T’Karra clasped her hands behind her back and began to speak.

“We are approaching the last known coordinates of the USS Arcturus, NCC-1660.”

Her voice carried the particular quality that Vulcans cultivated from childhood: perfectly modulated, stripped of inflection, each word placed with the precision of a surgeon’s instrument. She did not raise it. She did not need to. The bridge fell quiet the way a room does when someone speaks who expects to be heard.

“Miranda-class. Eleven months and six days ago, the Arcturus transmitted its final routine telemetry while conducting a long-range cartographic survey of unmapped subspace boundary regions.”

T’Karra moved her gaze across the bridge, station to station, officer to officer. Vex had turned from his console, those sharp eyes already calculating. Thon stood at the tactical station with the coiled stillness of a predator listening for prey. Malevolant watched her with that particular Orion attentiveness that could mean anything from genuine interest to professional assessment. Brex — Brex had one hand on his console, one hand adjusting a diagnostic display, and his third hand resting on the armrest of his chair with the studied indifference of someone who had heard ten thousand briefings and found most of them wanting.

“Crew complement seventy-eight. Commanding officer, Captain Theron Vazic.”

She paused. Not for effect — T’Karra did not do things for effect. She paused because the next sentence required its own space, the way a crack in a hull required vacuum.

“There was no distress signal. No debris field. No ion trail. The Arcturus simply ceased all communication.”

Something shifted in the bridge. Not a sound, not a movement. A quality of attention. Vex’s fingers stilled on his console. Thon’s antennae — those extraordinarily sensitive barometers of Andorian alertness — swept forward by a fraction of a degree. Even Brex’s third hand stopped its idle drumming.

“Fourteen search missions have found nothing.”

T’Karra registered each reaction with the clinical detachment her training demanded. The Andorian’s jaw tightened — threat assessment, the Security officer’s reflex. The Orion’s expression smoothed into careful neutrality — the mask of someone who understood that information was currency, and she had just received a substantial deposit. The Kzinti doctor, Healer, sat motionless at the medical station, but T’Karra could feel the faintest brush against her mental shields — the telepathic equivalent of someone leaning forward.

Fourteen missions. The number itself was significant. Starfleet did not commit fourteen separate search operations without cause, and it did not abandon the search after fourteen without reason. Something about the Arcturus’s disappearance had troubled Command enough to keep looking, and troubled them enough to stop.

“Three days ago, a Vulcan long-range sensor array at Station T’Karath detected a faint automated beacon matching the Arcturus’s transponder frequency.”

She let the implication settle.

“Originating from a void corridor that, by every standard measurement, contains nothing of note.”

“A void corridor,” Brex said. It was not a question. His deep voice carried the particular weight of someone who had been an engineer since before the Federation existed in its current form. “So we’re flying into empty space looking for a ship that vanished without a trace, based on a signal from a region of nothing.” His third hand resumed its drumming. “Lovely. This feels like politics to me, and I am entirely too old for politics.”

T’Karra did not respond to the comment directly. A Vulcan did not dignify complaints with acknowledgment. Instead, she continued her measured circuit of the bridge, her robes shifting against the deck plating with a sound like old parchment — the only soft thing on a bridge of hard edges and harder light.

“The Vulcan Science Academy requested the Enterprise specifically.”

That drew a look from Vex. A human raised on Tellar Prime understood what requesting a specific ship meant: someone with influence was pulling threads. T’Karra met his gaze for a fraction of a second — long enough for the young science officer to understand that she was the thread.

“Captain Kirk has authorised this investigation and placed me in acting command for the approach.”

She stopped. Stood at the centre of the bridge, hands still clasped behind her back, shoulders squared beneath robes that had no rank insignia, no department colour, no Starfleet delta. A civilian giving orders to officers on the Federation flagship.

“Our objectives are threefold. Determine the status of the Arcturus and her crew. Recover all scientific data from the cartographic survey. And ascertain why a Starfleet vessel vanished without trace.”

The words were precise, clinical, arranged in descending order of urgency. Seventy-eight people first. Data second. Answers third. T’Karra was aware that this ordering revealed something about her that Vulcans preferred to keep concealed — a hierarchy of priorities that suggested the crew mattered more to her than the mission. A logician would have placed the data recovery first, as the most achievable objective. She had not.

She filed the observation away for later examination.

The deck shuddered.

It was gentle — barely perceptible, a vibration that travelled through the soles of her boots and up through her legs. The Enterprise dropping out of warp. A sensation she had experienced hundreds of times aboard dozens of vessels, and yet this time it carried a wrongness she could not immediately categorise. The engines wound down from their high harmonic whine, and in the sudden relative quiet, the bridge felt larger. Emptier.

“We have arrived,” Vex said, his fingers moving across the science console. “Coordinates confirmed. We are—”

He stopped.

The viewscreen shifted. Stars — or rather, the absence of stars. The void corridor was exactly what its name suggested: a stretch of space so empty, so devoid of stellar matter, that the viewscreen seemed to display a fault rather than a view. No nebulae. No distant suns. No dust clouds catching the light of far-off stars. Just darkness, pure and absolute, the kind of darkness that existed before creation and would exist after the last star burned to ash.

And in the centre of that darkness, a ship.

The USS Arcturus hung in the void like a body suspended in deep water. Miranda-class — that familiar profile, the rollbar above the saucer section, the twin nacelles glowing with a light that had no business being there. Running lights traced her hull in faint blue-white pinpricks, each one a tiny defiance against the surrounding dark. She was intact. Undamaged. Her navigational deflector pulsed with a slow, steady rhythm.

She looked, T’Karra thought, like a ship waiting for her crew to come back.

The thought was irrational. She dismissed it.

“NCC-1660,” Vex confirmed quietly. “The Arcturus.”

No one spoke. The bridge of the Enterprise — that storied bridge where Kirk had stared down Klingon warbirds and talked gods out of their fury — was silent in a way it had never been. Not the silence of discipline or readiness. The silence of six people looking at something that should not exist. A ship that had been missing for nearly a year, sitting in empty space with its lights on and its engines cold, as though it had simply decided to stop and wait.

T’Karra’s hands, still clasped behind her back, tightened against each other. The pressure grounded her. A Vulcan technique — physical sensation to anchor the mind when logic threatened to give way to something older, something that lived in the brainstem where even Surak’s teachings could not reach.

Seventy-eight people. And the ship was still here, still powered, still breathing in the dark.

Where were they?

The console sang to her in frequencies most species couldn’t hear.

Malevolant’s fingers moved across the sensor array with the unhurried precision of someone who had learned, long before Starfleet Academy, that the first scan was never the important one. The first scan told you what was there. The second told you what was missing. And what was missing always mattered more.

She was aware of every person on the bridge without looking at any of them. T’Karra stood at the centre, radiating that particular brand of Vulcan composure that an Orion recognized instantly as a mask worn so long it had fused to the bone. Thon occupied the security station with the coiled stillness of an Andorian who hadn’t decided yet whether to fight or wait. Brex had plugged his neural interface into the engineering console the moment they’d dropped from warp, and two of his three hands were already dancing across controls while the third scratched absently at his chin. Vex sat at the science station, fingers hovering above his panel, watching the Arcturus on the viewscreen with the expression of a man who’d found a new equation and wasn’t sure yet whether it would balance.

The Arcturus hung in the dark like a held breath.

“Scanning for crew complement,” Malevolant said, and she didn’t ask permission because she didn’t need it. The Enterprise’s sensor array was a beautiful instrument — Starfleet’s finest, calibrated to detect a single humanoid biosignature through three decks of duranium. She tuned the sweep to full biological spectrum: humanoid, non-humanoid, active, dormant. Then she initiated the scan and watched the data cascade down her screen.

Nothing.

She adjusted the sweep, widened the parameters, checked the calibration. The Enterprise’s sensors were functioning within optimal range. She was reading the Arcturus clearly — its hull composition, its power grid, the trace elements of its atmospheric recyclers. The ship was right there in front of her, close enough to read its name on the hull.

Zero life signs.

The number sat on her screen like a closed door. Not reduced. Not faint. Zero. Seventy-eight people assigned to a Miranda-class vessel, and the sensors couldn’t find a single heartbeat, a single breath, a single cell dividing in a single body.

“No life signs aboard the Arcturus,” she reported, and kept her voice level because that was what the bridge needed right now. Clinical. Professional. “None.”

“None?” Thon’s antennae rotated sharply toward her. “Define none.”

“Zero humanoid biosignatures. Zero non-humanoid biosignatures.” She paused, let the silence do the work for a moment. “Crew complement should be seventy-eight.”

The bridge absorbed this. Malevolant felt the shift in the room — the way T’Karra’s stillness deepened, the way Vex’s hovering fingers finally settled against his console, the way Thon’s hand drifted a centimetre closer to the phaser on his hip without conscious thought. She catalogued each reaction the way she catalogued everything: automatically, compulsively, because an Orion who stopped reading the room was an Orion who didn’t survive the room.

“Continue scanning, Ensign,” T’Karra said. “Ship status.”

Malevolant was already doing it. She swept the Arcturus stem to stern, system by system, and what she found made the absence of life signs worse by degree. Shields down. Weapons cold. Navigation systems on standby. Life support functional but minimal — enough oxygen cycling to keep someone alive, but only just, as if the ship were breathing in its sleep.

The running lights on the viewscreen confirmed it. They should have been bright, steady, pulsing with the confidence of a Starfleet vessel on active duty. Instead they glowed faintly, dimly, like embers in a fire that should have gone out months ago but somehow hadn’t.

“Shields are down. Life support is running at minimum. The ship is powered but dormant.” She hesitated for the first time, and she did not like hesitating. “It’s not a derelict. It reads more like—”

She stopped herself before she said waiting. That was instinct talking, not data. But the thought lodged in her gut and stayed there.

“Like what?” Vex asked from the science station, because of course he caught the pause.

“Like something mothballed it,” she said instead. “Carefully.”

She had one more question she could pull from the data, and she knew already what to ask. This was where her training and her nature intersected — the Starfleet officer following procedure and the Orion following the cold thread of suspicion to its source. Seventy-eight people don’t vanish. They die, and they leave bodies. They evacuate, and they leave transporter logs. They are taken, and they leave evidence of struggle. They don’t simply stop existing.

“Scanning for biomatter,” she said. “Residual organic traces.”

The console processed the sweep. Malevolant watched the results populate and felt something cold settle against the base of her spine.

Nothing. No blood. No tissue. No hair follicles caught in air recyclers, no skin cells in the carpeting, no organic residue of any kind. The ship was clean — not decontaminated, not scrubbed, but clean, as though seventy-eight human beings had never set foot inside it. As though they had been unwritten from the physical record of the vessel they’d served on for years.

She sat with that for two seconds before reporting it, because she needed those two seconds to make sure her voice wouldn’t betray her.

“No biomatter. No organic traces of any kind. The crew didn’t die on that ship. They weren’t removed from it.” She turned her chair to face T’Karra, because this deserved eye contact. “They’re simply gone.”

At the science station, Vex was already moving. “I’m accessing the Arcturus computer — pulling ship’s logs.”

Malevolant watched him work from the corner of her eye while she ran secondary sweeps she already knew would come back empty. Vex had quick hands and a quicker mind; he’d grown up on Tellar Prime among people who argued for sport, and he’d chosen physics instead — the one language that didn’t lie. She trusted his competence the way she trusted the Enterprise’s hull integrity: implicitly, without needing to think about it.

His fingers flickered across the science console. “I’m in. The computer’s responding to remote query. Logs are—” He stopped. His hands went still.

Malevolant’s attention sharpened. “Vex.”

“The logs are here.” He said it slowly, the way someone describes a landscape that’s shifting beneath their feet. “I can see them scrolling. Duty rosters, department reports, navigational records. But—” He leaned closer to his screen. “They’re disappearing.”

“Clarify.”

“I’m watching them vanish. Right now. In real-time.” His voice had gone flat with the particular calm of someone who understood exactly what he was looking at and wished he didn’t. “The Arcturus logs are being deleted while I read them. Faster than I can capture them.”

The bridge went quiet in the way that bridges do when the situation crosses a threshold everyone feels but no one names. Malevolant’s hands tightened on her console. Someone — something — on that dormant ship knew they were reading its records, and it was erasing them. Not hiding. Not encrypting. Erasing, with the confidence of something that didn’t care if they noticed.

“Can you preserve any of them?” T’Karra asked.

Vex shook his head once. “They’re gone.”

Brex spoke for the first time in minutes, and when he did, it wasn’t in the voice of a man browsing antique repair manuals. Two of his three hands had gone rigid against his console, the neural interface at his temple blinking with a steady amber pulse that Malevolant had learned meant heavy data throughput.

“I’ve been scanning the Arcturus warp field,” he said. “There isn’t one. But there’s something coming from the deflector dish.”

Malevolant shifted her scan to the subspace band and saw it immediately — a low-frequency emission, rhythmic, emanating from the Arcturus deflector array. Not a distress signal. Not a beacon. Something organic in its cadence, something that pulsed and paused and pulsed again with a regularity that felt deeply, fundamentally wrong coming from a starship’s deflector.

Brex mapped the rhythm on his screen. Malevolant watched the waveform take shape — peak, trough, peak, trough, with a brief double flutter that repeated every 0.8 seconds.

“It’s a sinus rhythm,” Brex said quietly, and all three of his hands went still at once. In the months she’d served with him, Malevolant had never seen all three go still at the same time. “A human heartbeat. The deflector dish of the Arcturus is pulsing in time with a human heartbeat.”

The cold thing at the base of Malevolant’s spine climbed three vertebrae. She looked at the Arcturus on the viewscreen — dark, dormant, its running lights breathing faintly in the void — and for the first time saw it not as a derelict but as something wearing a derelict’s skin.

It reads more like waiting, she thought again. And this time she didn’t correct herself.

“I can get into their visual systems.” Thon’s voice cut through the silence like a blade through silk — sharp, deliberate, Andorian. He was already working at the security station, his jaw set with the expression of a man who’d rather kick down a door than stand outside it listening to what was behind it. “Same access pathway Vex used. I have Starfleet security override codes. If their screens are powered, I can bring up their viewscreen.”

“Do it,” T’Karra said.

Thon’s hands moved with a soldier’s efficiency. Malevolant heard the chirp of authentication protocols, the hum of a remote connection establishing itself across the gap between two ships, and then the Enterprise’s main viewscreen split — their own forward view on one side, and on the other, a feed from the Arcturus bridge.

The Arcturus viewscreen showed the Enterprise from the other side of the dark. Their ship, bright and sharp-edged against the void, the only real source of light in this starless corridor of space. The image had the flat, cold quality of an old-style CRT display — black background, green text rendering. The Enterprise sat in the centre of it like something pinned to a specimen board.

And below the image, in green text that hadn’t been there a moment ago, four lines:

I HAVE MAPPED 78 OF YOU.
YOU ARE BEAUTIFUL.
THERE ARE MORE OF YOU NOW.
I WOULD LIKE TO CONTINUE.

Malevolant read it and felt the words land somewhere below her ribs, in the place where instinct lived. Mapped. Not killed. Not consumed. Not abducted. Mapped. Like a cartographer tracing coastlines. Like a collector cataloguing specimens. Like something that had taken seventy-eight people apart with the tenderness of a lover memorising a body by touch.

You are beautiful.

The bridge was silent. Six officers staring at four lines of green text and the yawning darkness behind them.

“Denied,” Thon said flatly, and the word broke the spell like a stone through glass.

Vex let out a breath. Brex unplugged his neural interface with the deliberate care of someone removing their hand from a hot surface. Malevolant’s fingers were still on her console and she realized they hadn’t moved in eight seconds, which was seven seconds longer than she’d ever frozen in her life.

There are more of you now. It could see them. Whatever it was, whatever had taken the Arcturus crew with such clinical intimacy that it hadn’t left a single cell behind — it could see the Enterprise. It knew they were here. It was pleased.

“You didn’t mention this was going to get creepy,” Vex said, in the tone of someone filing a formal complaint with the universe.

T’Karra stood very still at the centre of the bridge. Malevolant watched the Vulcan’s composure hold, crack, and reassemble itself in the space of two seconds. The crack was tiny — a tightening around the eyes, a fractional shift in her breathing — but it was there, and Malevolant saw it, because seeing things other people missed was what kept an Orion alive.

“Fine,” T’Karra said, and her voice carried an edge that was not quite emotion and not quite its suppression. “Figure out the creepy human-eating monster on your own.”

She paused. The bridge waited.

“Or,” T’Karra continued, and something in her tone shifted from frustration to the cold clarity of decision, “let’s form an away team.”

Malevolant looked at the green text one more time. I would like to continue. Four words that reframed everything — the empty ship, the absent crew, the heartbeat pulsing from a deflector dish. This wasn’t a disaster scene. It wasn’t a crime scene. It was an invitation.

She turned back to her console and began calculating transporter coordinates for the Arcturus.

Thon’s hand was already on his phaser.

He hadn’t drawn it — not consciously — but somewhere between I have mapped 78 of you and I would like to continue, his fingers had found the type-2 on his hip and closed around the grip like a prayer. An Andorian prayer, the kind spoken through clenched teeth. Uzaveh, guide my aim. And if there is nothing to aim at, guide my fists.

T’Karra was already pointing. At Malevolant first, because the Orion had found the empty crew complement. At Vex, because the science officer had watched the logs devour themselves. At Brex, because the old Edosian had heard the deflector singing. At Thon, because —

Because he was security. Because that was what security did. They walked toward the thing that ate seventy-eight people and they walked toward it first.

Her finger found Healer last. The Kzinti doctor had been silent through the scanning, standing at the medical station with her ears flat against her skull — the feline equivalent of a human’s hair standing on end. She met T’Karra’s gesture with a slow nod. A doctor goes where there might be wounded. Even when the wounded have been gone for eleven months.

“All of us?” Vex said, and Thon heard the crack in his voice that the young lieutenant was trying very hard to hide.

“I’m nicer than Kirk.” T’Karra’s tone was utterly flat, utterly Vulcan, and utterly devastating. “Therefore you may all come with me. Including —” she glanced toward the two security officers stationed at the aft consoles, whose names Thon should have known but didn’t, because they’d rotated onto the night shift three days ago and he’d been meaning to review the personnel files — “the two officers who shall serve as additional tactical support for whatever is over there consuming human beings.”

One of them — young, red-shirted, the kind of face that hadn’t yet learned what a bad day in Starfleet actually looked like — swallowed hard enough that Thon heard it from across the bridge.

Brex hadn’t moved from his engineering station. Three hands continued their work across the console, though Thon noticed that only two were doing anything useful. The third was tapping a rhythm against the panel’s edge — the same rhythm, Thon realised with a cold twist in his gut, as the heartbeat from the Arcturus deflector.

“Brex,” T’Karra said.

“I heard you.”

“Brex.”

The Edosian’s ancient eyes — four hundred years of seeing things go wrong in every conceivable way — lifted from his console. “I’m an expert in ancient technology. Not in ancient horrors.”

“If you come on this away mission,” T’Karra said, and something in her voice shifted into a register that Thon had never heard from a Vulcan — something that might, in a less disciplined species, have been called wheedling, “I will personally assist you in repairing your hi-fi system.”

Two of Brex’s hands stopped moving. The third kept tapping. “The Marantz?”

“The Marantz.”

Brex stood. “Well. You should have led with that.”

The turbolift doors opened with their familiar pneumatic sigh, and Captain James T. Kirk walked onto the bridge.

There was no other way to describe it. Other officers entered. Kirk walked onto. He moved like a man who had been told the universe was dangerous and had taken it as a personal challenge. His gold tunic was regulation-perfect, his hair was regulation-imperfect, and his smile — that smile — managed to convey both supreme confidence and genuine warmth in a way that made Thon’s antennae itch with something he refused to identify as admiration.

Kirk’s eyes swept the bridge in a single practised arc. He took in the viewscreen with its ghost ship, the away team assembling near the turbolift, the two pale redshirts, and T’Karra standing beside the chair he was about to claim. His eyebrows rose a fraction — acknowledging the situation, the danger, and the Vulcan’s authority in one economical gesture.

“Enjoy your mission,” Kirk said, with a nod that was almost a bow. “I’ll take the ship.”

He walked to the command chair and sat in it the way water fills a glass — completely and without effort.

T’Karra watched him settle into the seat. “You’re taking command of the ship. Please don’t take the ship.”

Kirk grinned at her. Just grinned. The kind of grin that launched a thousand Starfleet recruitment posters and at least three interstellar incidents.

“They’re warming up the transporters in transporter room two,” he said. “Only six can go.” A beat. “Good luck.”

T’Karra turned away. Thon, who was learning to read Vulcans the way a hunter reads weather, caught the nearly imperceptible tightening around her eyes.

This is why Vulcans don’t like humans.

The corridor to deck seven was the longest walk Thon had ever taken on the Enterprise, and he’d once chased an escaped tribble colony from the arboretum to the cargo bay at a dead sprint.

They moved in formation without anyone calling for it. T’Karra at the front, because she’d claimed command and Thon respected that even when it made his security instincts howl. Malevolant beside her, the Orion’s hand resting on her own phaser with the casual ease of someone who’d been armed since before she could walk. Vex behind them, tricorder already open, scanning nothing, needing the comfort of data. Healer padding along in that silent Kzinti way that made her footsteps sound like a rumour. Brex bringing up the rear with the two redshirts, three hands gesturing as he explained something about subspace harmonics that was almost certainly going over their heads but was keeping them from thinking about what they were walking toward.

And Thon. Thon walked where security chiefs walk — at the edge, where he could see everything and reach everyone. His phaser was in his hand now, openly, and nobody said a word about it.

The Enterprise hummed around them, alive with the subsonic vibrations of a healthy ship. Thon focused on that sound. Held it in his mind like a talisman. Because in twenty minutes, the sound around him would be different. The Arcturus would have its own voice — the tick of cooling metal, the whisper of minimal life support, the rhythm of that impossible heartbeat pulsing through the deflector dish.

Seventy-eight crew. Not dead — the scans would have found remains. Not escaped — there was nowhere to go. Just gone. Mapped, according to the entity. Found beautiful.

His antennae tracked the ambient temperature of the corridor. Seventeen point four degrees. Standard. Normal. The kind of number that shouldn’t make his skin crawl, but did, because somewhere on the Arcturus the environmental controls were maintaining the same temperature for an audience of zero.

Preserve and protect. His oldest value. The one he’d carried from the ice plains of Andoria to the Academy to the Enterprise. But you couldn’t protect people from something that left no trace, no wound, no body. You couldn’t stand between your crew and a threat that said you are beautiful and meant it.

The doors to transporter room two were open. The pad glowed with that familiar blue-white shimmer — six circles of light arranged in two rows. Thon had stood on transporter pads hundreds of times. He’d been disassembled into energy and reassembled on the other side and never once thought about what happened in between. The space between. The nowhere.

Now he thought about it.

Six pads. Six could go. That meant the two redshirts would stay behind. T’Karra’s eyes moved across the team — counting, calculating, making the kind of decision that Vulcans pretended was logical but was really about who you were willing to lose.

T’Karra, Vex, Malevolant, Brex, Healer, and Thon. The six of them who had seen the message. The six who knew what was waiting.

Thon stepped onto the pad. The surface was warm under his boots — the hum of the pattern buffer rising through the deck plating and into his bones. To his left, Malevolant checked her phaser setting one final time. To his right, Healer’s claws made soft clicks against the pad surface. Behind him, Brex muttered something about energy conversion ratios. Ahead, Vex stood rigid, his tricorder clutched to his chest like a shield. And T’Karra, at the front of the pad, stood perfectly still with her hands clasped behind her back and her chin raised and her ridiculous anachronistic glasses reflecting the transporter light.

The operator’s hands moved across the console. “Coordinates locked. Arcturus main corridor, deck one.”

Thon tightened his grip on the phaser. He thought of seventy-eight people who had stood in corridors just like this one. Who had gone about their duties, eaten in their mess hall, slept in their quarters, lived their small and irreplaceable lives — until something found them beautiful.

“Energize,” T’Karra said.

The light took them.
